    Strike takes a number, ATM, or a delta — 25d, 10dp, -25d. A bare 25d takes its wing from the option type. Expiry takes a date or a tenor (1M, 3M), resolved through spot and the delivery date on the pair's holiday calendar. Fwd pts are added to spot as points / pip. Premiums are per unit of base notional in the term currency, undiscounted — this model carries no rate curve. Amounts scale by the notional in millions.
    Product: vanilla, digital (European, pays at expiry), one_touch / no_touch (continuous monitoring; price is a fraction of the payout, so Notional is the payout in millions). Digital ramp % is the call-spread width used to replicate the digital — zero is the unhedgeable limit, wider is what you can actually run. Overhedge shifts the barrier: extend in parallel for the whole life, bend_front full at inception tapering to nothing at expiry, bend_back the reverse. A bent barrier is time-dependent and has no closed form, so it is simulated and reports its Monte Carlo standard error. Buffers are applied against you: selling gets the conservative shift, buying the other way. Leave Spot blank to take spot and interpolated forward points from the feed.

    Futures price is the forward the curve is fitted around, in the contract's own quoted units; it is not taken from the FX feed, because a listed future is its own market. Expiry carries a time of day — use the exchange's cut, not midnight, or the curve is fitted at the wrong maturity.
    The fit is a vega-weighted least squares over three SABR parameters at fixed beta, swept over the whole admissible box before polishing, so it does not depend on a starting guess. With exactly three strikes it is an exact interpolation and the zero residuals mean nothing — the panel says so.
    Comparison. When the contract maps to a pair in the book, the marked surface is read at the same physical strikes. For a contract quoted the other way up (6J is USD per JPY) strikes are reciprocated and the wings swap sides; volatility itself is unchanged by that inversion. The delta points in the right-hand table are the book's delta strikes with both curves read off them, so the risk reversals below them are a slope comparison at fixed strikes, not two quotes in two conventions.
    Caveats this model does not correct for: exchange settlement volatilities on American-style options are not European volatilities; a future is not a forward once rates move with the underlying; and the exchange's settlement time is rarely an FX cut.

    Carry and rolldown. Every tenor is revalued after the horizon at a fixed absolute strike — the option you own keeps its strike while both the maturity and the forward move under it. The roll splits into the slide along the term structure (same moneyness, shorter maturity) and the slide across the smile (same maturity, forward moved), so the forward curve's contribution is separable rather than buried. /atm is the roll per year as a fraction of the at-the-money level. Without a forward feed for the pair the strike is held in moneyness instead of price, the smile slide is zero by construction, and the row says so.
    Fair value. Hold the T at-the-money option for the horizon h and delta hedge it. The mark slides by roll, worth vega(T−h)·roll; the gamma against theta earns roughly h/T of the option's life at σ_realized − σ_implied. Setting the two to cancel gives fair = realized + roll·(T/h)·vega(T−h)/vega(T), and richness is the implied less that. Positive means the market is charging more than realized volatility and the carry together justify. The roll here is always the at-the-money roll, whatever the carry table above is displaying.
    Realized against implied. Realized volatility is annualised by the same volatility time the model integrates variance with — weekends near zero, holidays reduced, the intraday profile not flat. Calendar and 252-day annualisations are shown beside it because they answer a different question and are typically a tenth lower. Skew and kurtosis need the same care in the other direction: the realized figures are daily, the implied ones are for the whole return to expiry, so the daily numbers are projected onto each tenor (skew/√n, kurtosis/n) before being compared. Both are shown, with standard errors — a skew inside one standard error of zero is not a skew.
    Cross triangle. The at-the-money row has an exact answer and gets one: the variance triangle the book itself is built on. The risk reversal and butterfly have no exact answer from two marginals and a correlation, so each leg's whole marked density is read off its smile, the two are tied together with a Gaussian copula at the marked correlation, and the cross's smile is integrated out. That assumes a dependence structure the market does not quote and ignores the change of measure between the legs' domestic currencies; the noise floor is the same machinery run on each leg alone, where it should reproduce the input exactly. A difference smaller than the noise is not a difference.
    Curve comparison. Any number of curves side by side, differenced against whichever one is marked base. A curve is the fitted surface at the cut and interpolation chosen above, the workbook quotes that went into it, one dated row of the historical workbook, or a curve pasted in — one line per tenor, 1M 8.20 -0.35 0.22 -0.60 0.75, with the columns in the order of the Show menu and anything after the first optional. A pasted curve's unit is decided once from its at-the-money column and refused if the levels straddle 1.0, because 0.35 is an ordinary risk reversal in points and an ordinary at-the-money in decimals. Dates take latest, a date, or an offset back from the last row (-30d, -3m); the row used is the last one on or before what was asked for, and the curve says which day it landed on. A tenor a source does not quote is blank rather than absent, and a curve that could not be built keeps its place and carries the reason. One caution the panel repeats: a historical sheet's butterfly column is whatever that desk quoted, while the book's is a market strangle.

    What this screen does, in the order it does it. The at-the-money curve is fitted to a target term structure — the tenors pinned on the marking screen, a pasted curve, or the mid of the at-the-money quotes in the paste. Then the four smile parameters are moved by an additive shift across the whole curve until the quoted wings are satisfied. Then the mid is shaded and a width is put round it. Each stage reports separately, so one that cannot run leaves the others alone.
    The wing fit is a hinge, not a least squares. There is no penalty anywhere inside a quoted bid and offer — the brief is that our mid falls inside the market, not on top of somebody's mid. Because a hinge has a flat bottom, a small pull toward the quoted mids picks one answer out of the many that satisfy the market; it is small enough never to override an actual violation. The shift is curve-wide on purpose: a handful of quotes does not determine a shape, and a shift that cannot reach a tenor says so in its residual instead of bending the surface to one quote.
    Conventions the paste decides once. The volatility unit comes from the whole paste's at-the-money and outright lines, never line by line — a small risk reversal read on its own looks exactly like a decimal at-the-money. A risk reversal with a direction word (EUR call over) is resolved against the pair; one without is read in the book's own convention, and the paste says so. An unqualified fly means whichever butterfly the selector above says, and the market strangle is the one the workbook marks.
    Shading the mid. A rich market and a long vega position both point the same way: both are reasons to want to sell, and you attract a seller's trade by shading the price down. Both are capped as a fraction of the width, so an axe can lean the price inside the market but cannot walk it out of the market on its own. Neither is applied to a risk reversal or a butterfly — a break-even against realized volatility and a vega position are statements about the level, and neither says where the skew belongs. Those rows say so.
    The knowledge bank is the desk's own file, kept beside the workbook. A spread sets the width, a floor is a minimum width, a shift moves the mid, and a note is prose that is shown beside the quote and never applied. The narrowest matching rule wins and every row names the rule that set it. There is no built-in default: a quote no rule matches gets no bid and no offer unless a fallback width is typed above, and says which it was.
    Nothing here touches the workbook. The fit reports and then puts the marks back. Tick keep the marks to leave them on the loaded book — still in memory only, and a reload discards them.
