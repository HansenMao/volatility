    How a leg is read Strike takes a number, ATM, or a delta — 25d, 10dp, -25d. A bare 25d takes its wing from the option type. Expiry takes a tenor (1W, 8d, 3M, 2Y, and the unit spelled out — 1wk, 3mth, 2yr, 10 days), one of the short-date codes (O/N, T/N, S/N, S/W), or a date in most of the ways one is written (2026-09-15, 15Sep26, 15 Sep 2026, September 15, 2026, 2026/09/15, 9/15/2026, 20260915) — and whatever you type it comes back as the one standard date. The year is optional: 15 Sep is the next fifteenth of September, counted forward from the valuation date, so a date already gone this year is next year's.
    A tenor is a settlement date first. It is added to the spot date, adjusted modified-following on the value-date calendars (with the end-of-month rule: off a spot that is its month's last value date, every month tenor settles on the last value date of its own month), and the expiry is the spot lag back from it on the pair's own holiday calendar — the market's construction, and the reason Settlement is the anchor rather than a consequence. Day tenors go the other way, because that is what they mean: O/N expires on the next business day and 8d on the eighth, each settling the spot lag later. Resolved once, so the leg holds the date rather than drifting with the clock.
    Settlement is that date, and it is a box: the date this option pays on, the date the desk confirms against, and the date the forward under it is a forward to. It fills itself from the pair's own calendar — the spot lag past the expiry, two business days for most pairs and one for USDCAD — and shows greyed while it is the calendar's. Type a date over it for a trade that settles somewhere the calendar would not have put it, and the forward is read there instead; the expiry does not move, so the volatility and the time to expiry are untouched. Empty the box to hand it back to the calendar. A date before the expiry is refused, and one that is not a value date for the pair is taken and said out loud. Spot, Swap and Forward are three boxes holding one identity — forward = spot + swap / pip. Spot and the swap points come from the feed at this leg's own settlement date — not its expiry: a forward is a price for a value date, and the two are a spot lag apart, which on a one-week option is a fifth of the points. The outright under them comes from the same place; all three are shown greyed while they are the feed's, and it is the outright that is priced. Type in any of them and the third follows — type a swap and the forward moves, type a forward and the swap moves to match. Empty a box to hand it back to the feed.
    The Results rows repeat none of this. What the pricer resolved is written back into the boxes above and is then what is priced — the expiry as the one standard date, the settlement date the calendar built from it, ATM or 25d as the absolute strike it solved to on the marks, and Auto as C or P. Type 25d in again to solve the strike afresh against the marks as they now stand. Price (pips) is the vanilla premium the way it is quoted: the term currency per unit of base through the pair's own pip — 10,000 on most pairs, 100 on a yen pair. Premium is per leg: it reads that leg's premium rows and its slice of the totals either as the forward (undiscounted) value the model makes or as spot, the premium as paid, discounted on the term currency's OIS feed rate to the premium date. A dash under spot is a term currency with no rate on the tab, and a small fwd / spot tag beside each cell says which of the two it is. An exotic's price is a fraction of its payout rather than a price per unit of base, so it is shown as a percentage instead. Smile cash gamma is the base, in millions, the delta hedge moves by for a one per cent move in spot, taken along the smile like the delta above it. Amounts scale by the notional in millions.
    Product: vanilla, digital (European, pays at expiry), one_touch / no_touch (continuous monitoring; price is a fraction of the payout, so Notional is the payout in millions). A row is only shown where the product uses it — no barrier or overhedge on a vanilla, no strike or type on a touch — because the model reads none of those there, and a box that is filled in and then ignored is a silent zero with a cursor in it. A row another column needs keeps its place and shows a dot in the columns that do not. Digital ramp % is the call-spread width used to replicate the digital — zero is the unhedgeable limit, wider is what you can actually run. Overhedge shifts the barrier: extend in parallel for the whole life, bend_front full at inception tapering to nothing at expiry, bend_back the reverse. A bent barrier is time-dependent and has no closed form, so it is simulated and reports its Monte Carlo standard error. Buffers are applied against you: selling gets the conservative shift, buying the other way.
    Refresh spot re-reads the feed file from disk, puts every market box back on the feed at each leg’s own settlement date — the levels you typed over included — and re-prices. It takes back the market and nothing else: a settlement date you have typed is a term of the trade, not a level, and stays where you put it. The Market row says what is left of the feed in each leg afterwards. It says how many legs it took back, because a hand-marked level that is now the file’s should not change in silence. A leg the feed has no pair for, or whose expiry will not resolve, is reported and left exactly as it was. A cross the file quotes only through its legs is filled from them, by the same triangle that prices it. To keep a level you have typed, do not press it: an emptied box is still the way to hand one box back on its own. Watch file only looks at when the file was last written and offers the refresh; nothing reloads underneath a price you are reading.

    Saved marks

    What a session file holdsEverything this session has marked — curve parameters, the correlation term structure, events, ATM and smile overwrites, any quote re-typed on the ATM term structure table, the market maker's wing shifts, the anchor switch, the band treatment, and any configuration tab applied in the Config window — written to a file beside the workbook. Marking a curve never touches the workbook: what a screen writes goes into this file. Start volkit with --session <file>, or press Load marks, to put them back; Reload workbook still discards everything and returns to the quotes on disk. Write to workbook saves the marks first and then writes the file into the loaded workbook — PARAMS curve rows, event rows and currency weights, the atm 1m / slog25 3m / term rho10 decay / shift rho25 / anchor rows and BANDS sheet the reader reads back, and the configuration tabs the Config window has applied — so nothing reaches the workbook that is not already written down in the session file. It is the one write. A peg band, a holiday, a kACE pillar or a wing ratio marked in the Config window is held with the marks and goes in here, in the same backup: there is one button that changes the book of record and one list of what it changed. Adding or removing a pair is the exception, and says so where it is done. A re-quoted RR or ST is the one thing that does not go into a row of its own: a quote is the sheet's own number, so it is written into the sheet's own cell and the pair tab is then the database it is being used as. That cell may have held a formula, and a number typed over one replaces it — which is why the backup matters here more than anywhere else. It asks before it writes, and keeps the workbook it replaced beside it as <name>.bak-<stamp>.xlsx: openpyxl does not carry images or charts through, so that backup is the way back. A copy instead of the original is volkit session --to-workbook.

    Which tenors are on this table, and what a smile row isThe tenors on this table are the ones CONFIG lists. A tenor CONFIG lists that the sheet does not quote is marked smile: its boxes are empty and the slanted numbers under them are what the fitted smile reads there, interpolated from the tenors either side.     Quotes take a block pasteQuotes take a block paste. Copy the columns out of a spreadsheet, click the box the block starts in and paste: it fills right across the quote columns and down the tenors from there, in one write and one refit. A leading tenor column is matched by name rather than by position, and a header row is dropped. A blank cell is left as it was — the way to take a quote off is still to empty its box — a derived wing is skipped, and anything past the last tenor or the last column is ignored and counted. One cell that is not a number refuses the whole block: half a pasted table is not a table anybody typed.

A tenor the tab cannot weight keeps its place and says so; it is not moved. Show reads, Apply writes: what it writes is per-tenor ATM overwrites, counted in this card’s heading and cleared by the button above.

    How the boxes are read The pair, the cut and the interpolation are the ones above. Expiry takes a tenor (1W, 8d, 3M, 1wk, 3mth) or a date in any of the ways one is written, with the year optional (15 Sep is the next one of it), and Strike takes a number or ATM. Delta is the same point on the smile asked for the other way round — 25, 10p, -25, or the pricing screen's own 25d / 10dp; a bare delta is the call wing. Fill in either one: typing in one box clears the other, and the answer puts what it resolved to into the empty box's greyed-out hint, so the strike and the delta of the volatility being read are both on the card whichever way it was asked for. Expiry and strike are the pricing screen's boxes, read by the same code, so the two screens cannot understand one another differently; the delta comes back under the pair's own convention (premium adjusted for a USD-base pair).
    The forward comes from the market feed at this expiry, which is what puts an absolute strike on the same axis as the marks — and a cross the file quotes only through its legs is placed from them, by the same triangle that prices it. With no feed for the pair, ATM and a delta still answer, in K/F, and the card says so; an absolute strike is refused rather than quietly read as a ratio. A bare 25d names two strikes and is read on the call, as it is on the pricing tab; the strike box is where you say otherwise — 25dp or -25d is the put. There is no separate wing control, because that would be a second place to say one thing, and at ATM or an absolute strike there is nothing to say: the volatility there is one number for the call and the put.
    The boxes keep what you asked, and the line under them says what it resolved to — the expiry as the one standard date, 25d as the strike it solved to. This is a query and not a position: the pricing tab freezes a leg's strike so a re-price cannot silently re-solve it, and here re-solving is the whole point. Move a mark and the same question is asked again of the surface as it now stands.

    Events

    How a bump is builtEvery event lives on the EVENTS sheet of the workbook: one row per release, a column per currency and a column per pair. Times are Hong Kong time (UTC+8); the model works in UTC and converts. Bumps are vol points over the 24 hours following the release. A bump is the pair's two legs' weights added -- and a currency weight is shared, so typing one here moves every pair with that currency -- plus this pair's own Adj on top; only the parts are typed, the total is shown. The day column shows which volatility day the effect mostly lands in. Reload puts back this pair's rows as the workbook has them, discarding what the session marked. Nothing is written to the workbook until an export asks for it by name.

    Event weights

    What a weight doesThe currency side of the EVENTS sheet: what each release is worth on each currency, in vol points, one row per release. A pair's bump is its two legs added plus its own adjustment, so a weight here is shared -- applying it re-solves every pair with that currency. Each pair's own adjustment column is left exactly as it is.
    How a panel is read, and what it does not correct for One panel is one expiry / underlying combination. Paste the exchange's strike and volatility columns into the box — tabs, commas or spaces, with or without a header row, bid/ask or mid. Volatilities are read in points, as written — a table that sits below 1.0 is a managed pair, not a table of decimals — unless Vol unit says decimal. Every column choice the parser makes is reported under the table, and every line it cannot use is listed with the reason.
    Where the paste has a bid and an offer, both are kept beside the mid the curve is fitted to. bid/offer in the bar above draws them as two lines and marks every strike where the marked FX surface reads outside them — the question a mid cannot answer. The count is on the panel's own stat line whether or not the lines are drawn. A crossed or non-positive two-way is not a market: it is dropped and counted, and the row survives on its own mid where it has one. Setting Vol col by hand turns the two-way off, because that says which single column to believe.
    Contract is free text. The codes this build knows (6E, 6J, …) are offered as you type and bring their FX pair, their strike direction and their standard contract size with them; anything else is taken as typed, is marked typed wherever it is shown, and takes its pair, direction and size from this panel's own boxes. Nothing is guessed from the name. Two panels may share a code — the same contract at two expiries — and a position line then names the expiry as well.
    Futures price is the forward the curve is fitted around, in the contract's own quoted units; it is not taken from the FX feed, because a listed future is its own market. Expiry carries a time of day — use the exchange's cut, not midnight, or the curve is fitted at the wrong maturity.
    The fit is a vega-weighted least squares over three SABR parameters at fixed beta, swept over the whole admissible box before polishing, so it does not depend on a starting guess. With exactly three strikes it is an exact interpolation and the zero residuals mean nothing — the panel says so.
    Comparison. When the contract maps to a pair in the book, the marked surface is read at the same physical strikes. For a contract quoted the other way up (6J is USD per JPY) strikes are reciprocated and the wings swap sides; volatility itself is unchanged by that inversion. The delta points in the right-hand table are the book's delta strikes with both curves read off them, so the risk reversals below them are a slope comparison at fixed strikes, not two quotes in two conventions.
    Caveats this model does not correct for: exchange settlement volatilities on American-style options are not European volatilities; a future is not a forward once rates move with the underlying; and the exchange's settlement time is rarely an FX cut.

    Positions and risk

    How a position line is read, and what adds up One line is one option: contract, expiry, strike, C/P, contracts. Drop the leading columns when the screen has only one panel that can answer (strike, C/P, contracts), or put a header row on it and name the columns in any order. A strike of ATM is the panel's own forward. A quantity is signed: negative is short. A comma is a column boundary, so a size written 1,000 in a comma-separated paste is two columns and the line is refused — use tabs or spaces for that.
    Each line is priced against the panel above whose contract and expiry it names, and everything the greeks need — the forward, the expiry, the volatility at the strike — is that panel's. The one thing a fit never needed is the contract size, which is a field on each panel and defaults to the contract's standard (125,000 for 6E, 12,500,000 for 6J). A line that matches no panel, or two, keeps its place in the table and says which.
    Black-Scholes is the closed-form Black-76 sensitivity at the option's own volatility with that volatility held fixed as the future moves. Smile is the same position revalued on the fitted SABR curve, so the curve travels with the future and volatility is bumped by lifting the whole curve — normalised to a move measured at the forward. Both read the same volatility at the same strike, so the premiums are identical and the entire difference is in the sensitivities.
    What adds up, and it is three rows because three different things add. Per panel, every column adds. Per contract, every column still adds — the panels under one code are the same contract at different expiries — and that row appears only where there is more than one such panel. Two delivery months are not the same future, so a futures-equivalent total there is a net position and not a hedge ratio. Across contracts, only money adds: premium, vega, theta, the 1% delta and gamma money and volga, and only across contracts settling in the same currency. Every CME FX option settles in US dollars, which is why that is one row on an ordinary screen; a contract you typed need not, so the currency is derived from its pair and the totals are struck per currency. Futures-equivalent delta and gamma are never totalled across contracts — a euro future is not a yen future. Premiums and theta are undiscounted forward values, as everywhere else in volkit.

    What each card measures Relative value. Every expiry against every strike, with one score per cell. Positive is rich — the mark is above what the comparison says it should be, and that is the side to sell. The big figure is in volatility points — the weighted mean of this cell's signals, which is the number a mark is moved by and a price is made in — the small one under it is the break-even richness (level, shape and carry alone), and the tint is a second reading of the big number rather than the only one. Click a cell to take it apart. Five signals: level (marked at-the-money less realized), shape (your smile's shape at this strike less the shape the measured ρ and ν imply — the wings against what volatility actually did, not against another quote), carry (minus the roll and the forward carry, as the volatility they are worth: an option that rolls down has to be cheaper to break even, so a mark that did not fall is rich by the difference), history (where this cell sits in its own recent history) and, on a cross, triangle (your mark against what the two legs imply). The first three add: they are the fair-value break-even below moved out from the at-the-money to a strike, so their sum is the volatility-point figure in the small figure of the cell, and on the ATM column it is exactly the Rich column of the fair-value card. The other two answer a different question, so they are averaged into the score but kept out of that sum. Every signal is already in volatility points and the score is their weighted mean, so the whole card is in one unit. Beside each value the detail shows its z — the same difference against how much that cell's volatility usually moves, since half a point is a great deal on a one-year at-the-money and nothing on a one-week wing. That is reported and enters the score nowhere; it is measured over the history window, which is deliberately not the realized lookback: the lookback is matched to each tenor by default, and a month of a smooth one-month series is far too short to say how much anything moves. The lookback is Lookback (days) in the bar at the top, and it is the one level is measured over: leave it at match and each tenor is compared against its own length of realized volatility; type a number and every tenor is compared against that one window, so a 1M and a 2M mark read off the same realized number. The header of this card says which of the two you are running. The ρ and ν behind shape come off that same window and for the same reason — on the lookback they were blank at every short tenor, and at every tenor at once on a lookback under about a month, which left shape reading as a signal that never worked. A missing signal is renormalised away rather than counted as a zero, and so is the at-the-money's shape: it is zero there by statement rather than by measurement — the at-the-money is the level — so it is shown with its reason and kept out of the average, which is why an ATM cell is scored on less of the declared weight than the wings beside it. Faded cells were scored on less than half the declared weight. No history means no history signal and no z beside the others; the remaining signals are measured in volatility points and are scored on just the same.
    Three things the grid says rather than leaves to be inferred. level is printed once, in its own column beside the tenor: it is one number for the whole expiry and goes into all five cells, so it is one observation and not five agreeing ones. A tenor tagged carry has a forward that has drifted more than 0.8 standard deviations of its own volatility — z = |ln(F/S)|/(σ√T) — and past that line the position is mostly a carry trade in an option's clothes, with the carry signal carrying most of what the row says; hover the tenor for the numbers. Nothing is reweighted for you: the weights are yours, and a score that quietly changed its own recipe row by row would be unreadable. And the cell detail breaks the realized figure apart — what spot did, what the forward did, and fwd/spot, the ratio of the two. That ratio is the only honest answer to "is the carry supporting this volatility"; near one the points moved with spot and it makes no difference which you measure, well above one the points are carrying variance of their own. The level of the carry says nothing about that on its own, which is why it is not the column. A pair whose carry is large against a realized volatility that is also low in absolute terms is flagged once at the top as the shape of a managed float — both conditions, because a big rate differential alone describes USDJPY, and USDJPY is not managed.
    Carry and rolldown. Every tenor is revalued after the horizon at a fixed absolute strike — the option you own keeps its strike while both the maturity and the forward move under it. The roll splits into the slide along the term structure (same moneyness, shorter maturity) and the slide across the smile (same maturity, forward moved), so the forward curve's contribution is separable rather than buried. /atm is the roll per year as a fraction of the at-the-money level. Without a forward feed for the pair the strike is held in moneyness instead of price, the smile slide is zero by construction, and the row says so. The forward curve pays twice: Smile is it reaching your mark, in volatility points; Carry is it reaching your price — the option is worth V(F,K,σ,τ) and F itself has rolled — in basis points, with In vols the same over the position's vega. Whether you keep it is the hedge: in the outright forward to the option's own expiry the hedge rolls down exactly as the option does and the two cancel; in spot, as a desk hedges, nothing rolls on the hedge side and you keep it. The Δ smile is the delta you are actually running: this whole table is a fixed strike sliding under a moving forward, so the volatility that strike is marked at moves with it and dV/dF carries vega × dσ/dF along. Δ BS holds that volatility still — which is what a Black-Scholes delta is — and Skew is the difference, the whole of what the smile contributes. On a skewed surface it is large: a 25 delta put can run at 18 delta once the skew is in it. The at-the-money is a delta-neutral straddle in the Black-Scholes column only; its smile delta is not zero, because a straddle is long vega and the volatility moves with the forward — and that, not an error, is why the at-the-money row shows a little carry. Both columns are dV/dF in the term currency, which is what makes Δ BS × ΔF the carry and Δ smile × ΔF the whole of what the forward move does. They are deliberately not the premium-adjusted quoted delta on a pair that quotes one — that is a hedge ratio in the other currency and multiplying it by a move in the forward does not give money — so on USDJPY the 25 delta strike reads a little away from 0.25 here. Without a feed the carry columns are blank rather than zero.
    Fair value. Hold the T at-the-money option for the horizon h and delta hedge it. The mark slides by roll, worth vega(T−h)·roll; the gamma against theta earns roughly h/T of the option's life at σ_realized − σ_implied. Setting them to cancel gives fair = realized + (T/h)·[roll·vega(T−h) + carry]/vega(T) — the Roll value and Carry value columns — and richness is the implied less that. of which fwd is the forward curve reaching the mark and is first order; Carry value is it reaching the price, second order for a delta-neutral straddle and computed rather than assumed to be zero. Positive means the market is charging more than realized volatility and the carry together justify. The roll here is always the at-the-money roll, whatever the carry table above is displaying.
    Realized against implied. Realized volatility is measured on the forward to each tenor wherever the sheet quotes swap points, and the On column says which it used. A quoted volatility is the volatility of the forward you are struck against, so the swap points moving is realized volatility too (Pts % is that part alone, Spot % the same window without it). The points decaying by a day of carry is a known slide and is not counted — leaving it in would book the carry itself as volatility. A tenor the sheet does not quote has its carry interpolated between the pillars it does, so the term structure does not grow steps at whichever tenors happen to be quoted. Realized volatility is annualised by the same volatility time the model integrates variance with — weekends near zero, holidays reduced, the intraday profile not flat. Calendar and 252-day annualisations are shown beside it because they answer a different question and are typically a tenth lower. Skew and kurtosis need the same care in the other direction: the realized figures are daily, the implied ones are for the whole return to expiry, so the daily numbers are projected onto each tenor (skew/√n, kurtosis/n) before being compared. Both are shown, with standard errors — a skew inside one standard error of zero is not a skew.
    Wings as SABR shape. The moments above cannot answer the same question about the risk reversal and the butterfly: a quoted spread is not a moment, and a realized third moment is not a risk reversal. What both sides share is the two numbers a SABR smile is built from — ρ, the spot/volatility correlation a risk reversal is paid for, and ν, the vol of vol a butterfly is paid for. Marked is the pair a SABR smile would need to show your quoted at-the-money, risk reversal and butterfly; your surface is not SABR, so fit err says how far it is from any SABR smile at all. Measured is the same pair out of the history — under SABR with β = 1 the at-the-money is the state variable, so ρ is the correlation of daily spot returns with daily moves in the quoted at-the-money and ν is those moves annualised on the same volatility time. SABR has no mean reversion and real volatility does, so ν rises at short tenors on both sides and must not be blended across them; ν√t is the scale-free number that sets the shape.
    Cross triangle. The at-the-money row has an exact answer and gets one: the variance triangle the book itself is built on. The risk reversal and butterfly have no exact answer from two marginals and a correlation, so each leg's whole marked density is read off its smile, the two are tied together with a Gaussian copula at the marked correlation, and the cross's smile is integrated out. That assumes a dependence structure the market does not quote and ignores the change of measure between the legs' domestic currencies; the noise floor is the same machinery run on each leg alone, where it should reproduce the input exactly. A difference smaller than the noise is not a difference. The vega split differentiates the variance triangle instead of integrating it, and is exact: one unit of at-the-money vega on the cross is (σa+xσb)/σc units in the first leg and the mirror of it in the second, with x the signed correlation the triangle carries. Those are the two hedges. They are not shares and do not add to one: what is exact is that weighted by each leg's own volatility they account for the whole of the cross's (the triangle is homogeneous of degree one, so Euler's identity holds, and a test pins it). The correlation is degree zero and appears nowhere in that identity, which is why it is reported on its own: the per ρ column is what a whole unit of correlation is worth in cross volatility points, and no amount of leg vega touches it.

    Curve comparison

    How a panel and a curve are built Panels. One panel is one pair and two points in time: the five quoted numbers as they stand now, the same five as they stood then, and the change between them, tenor by tenor. Either end is any source the comparison below understands except a paste — the fitted surface, the workbook quotes, or a dated row of the historical workbook — so a panel is “the surface against last week's close”, “the surface against the quotes it was fitted to”, or two dated rows against each other, whichever question is being asked. The default is the surface against the historical row a week ago.
    Dates take latest, a date, or an offset back from the last row (-1w, -30d, -3m). The row used is the last one on or before what was asked for — a historical workbook has no weekend rows, and snapping forward would compare Friday against the following Monday — and each end reports the day it landed on. Two dated ends that land on the same row say so, because a column of zeros otherwise reads as a quiet market rather than as a comparison that never happened.
    An end that cannot be built does not empty the panel: the levels that could be read are still shown and the panel carries the reason it has no change. A tenor one end does not quote is a blank change, not a missing row. Highlight picks which of the five gets the levels column beside the changes; the changes themselves are always all five.
    The four All boxes in the control row are the same two ends written across every panel at once: pick a source or type a date there and each panel takes it, which is how a screen of pairs is moved from “against last week” to “against yesterday” without editing each one. They read back what the panels hold — blank when the panels do not agree — so they are a view of the list as much as a way to set it, and a panel edited on its own afterwards simply blanks the box again. The date boxes go quiet when no panel is reading the historical workbook, for the same reason a panel hides its own.
    Curve comparison. Any number of curves side by side, differenced against whichever one is marked base. A curve is the fitted surface at the cut and interpolation chosen above, the workbook quotes that went into it, one dated row of the historical workbook, or a curve pasted in — one line per tenor, 1M 8.20 -0.35 0.22 -0.60 0.75, with the columns in the order of the Show menu and anything after the first optional. A pasted curve is read in volatility points, as written: 8.20 is 8.20 points and 0.35 is a third of one, which is what a managed pair marks. The level never decides a unit here. Dates take latest, a date, or an offset back from the last row (-30d, -3m); the row used is the last one on or before what was asked for, and the curve says which day it landed on. A tenor a source does not quote is blank rather than absent, and a curve that could not be built keeps its place and carries the reason. One caution the panel repeats: a historical sheet's butterfly column is whatever that desk quoted, while the book's is a market strangle.

    What these knobs can and cannot determine
    This card is the only thing on the screen that moves a mark Every vol mark that moves, moves here. The other two buttons on this tab read the curve and never touch it: Check Market says where the marks sit against a pasted run, Quote makes a two-way. There used to be a Fit button beside them that also moved the curve and the wings, which meant two ways to re-mark and only one of them written down. There is now one card, one journal and one place to look.
    Propose is the agent: it reads the market paste and the target curve above, decides which knobs to free and how far to trust what this desk has done to proposals like this one, runs the fit and hands back numbers. With the market box empty and score against the archive ticked, the archive is the market: the two agents confer — the quoting agent’s archived levels are the targets, the marking agent proposes, the quoting agent critiques, what broke is reweighted and it proposes again, for at most Rounds rounds — and the best round is the proposal, answered with the same four buttons. That was volkit mark confer, a command with no button. Fit my way is the same two fitters with exactly the knobs ticked here instead — the desk driving them, which is what makes an edited answer possible.
    The four answers. Accept takes the proposal as the marks the check and the quote stand on. Take the plan and fit sets the knob boxes to the agent’s plan and runs it, so you can adjust, press Fit my way again, and then record my fit as the edit — the only row in the journal where the tool’s opinion and the desk’s sit side by side on the same morning. Reject records that the surface was left where it was, which is also worth knowing.
    Nothing here reaches the workbook. A proposal is numbers the browser holds; the journal is the only file written. Tick keep the marks to leave what you accepted, edited or fitted on the loaded book — still in memory only, and a reload discards it.

    Quoting agent’s archive

    What the quoting agent learns, and where its answer isIts answer is the Quote button’s. Every row of the quote sheet carries what it learned: the width the archive has seen the instrument shown at (the second rung of the width ladder, below a bank rule and above the fallback tier), its verdict on the width the bank would show, the market’s recent level as a check on the mark, and the client’s record on that instrument. This card is the archive itself: what it holds, and the four ways to put more into it — the watched folders, DTCC, the run pasted above, and Record as shown on the quote sheet, which is where a client’s record starts. The settings here are the quote’s too, so the two never disagree about what the archive holds. There used to be a Suggest button here that compared widths on its own; its columns moved onto the quote sheet, beside the price they are about.
    The printed tape is under the widths: the dissemination files publish what printed and never who bought — no buyer, no seller, no aggressor flag, by design — so a side here is inferred. Each print is turned into the volatility it implies, against the forward of its own date, and read as paid above our mark and given below it, with anything inside Flow tol counted as unclear rather than pushed to a side. The mark is the surface as it stands now and not as it stood when the trade printed, which is why the half-life above is short and is the tape’s too. Size is vega, in the base currency per volatility point — the unit the axe is typed in. Trades published at the dissemination cap are counted in what printed and left out of the vega. It reaches the price as the Flow column on the quote sheet, and only when Flow wt is above zero; ask the record “who has been paying in the 1M” for the same reading in words.

    Ask the record

    What the third agent can and cannot doThe third agent, and the only one that cannot change anything. It reads the archive, the journal, the knowledge bank and the surface, and answers in facts, each tagged with where it came from: widths, levels, what printed, which side the tape has been on (“who has been paying in the 1M”, judged against the same mark the quote leans on), a client’s record (“is Fund A a buyer or a seller of the 3M ATM”, the same record the quote applies), the journal and the marks. It takes the archive card’s evidence settings above. Ask it to fetch, re-mark, record or quote and it names the button that does that. “And the 1M?” keeps the topic of the question before.

    The three buttons, and how a run is read Three buttons, because these are three jobs, and only one of them moves a mark. Check Market reads the market box and says where we are marked against it: through their bid or their offer, near a side, or in line, with the distance in vol points and in units of their own width. It moves nothing. Quote reads the box below it, where you write what you are being asked for, and makes a two-way in each line: the model’s mid, the bank’s width round it, and the leans. It moves nothing either, and it answers instantly, so it can be pressed as often as the phone rings. The Marking agent card on the right is the one place a curve or a wing changes — the agent’s proposal, or your own fit run from the same card, answered and written into the journal.
    There used to be a Fit button here. It read the market box, moved the at-the-money curve to a target term structure and shifted the four smile parameters until the quoted wings were satisfied — which is exactly what the marking agent does, more carefully and with a record of it. Two ways to re-mark a curve on one screen meant half the re-marks went unlearned. The fitters themselves are unchanged; what changed is that they are reached from one card.
    What the check stands on. With stand on the agent’s marks ticked, the numbers the marking card last handed back — an accepted proposal, or your own fit — are sent with the request and put on the surface for the length of that one call; untick it, or run nothing on that card, and the check and the quote read the marks as they stand. Both say which of the two it was, every time, because a market checked against this morning’s proposal and one checked against last night’s marks must never read the same. Nothing is left on the book either way — that is keep the marks, on the marking card, and it is a separate decision.
    Edge tol is how far inside a quoted two-way still counts as near its edge, as a fraction of the width: a quarter by default, so a mark in the outer quarter of somebody’s market is flagged amber before it is through rather than after. Zero leaves only what is actually outside. A choice price has no inside and is never called near an edge. A line the surface cannot be read at is not checked — a message, never a pass.
    The request box takes no prices. The same words as the market box with the price left off: 1M ATM in 100mm, 3M 25d RR, 6M 1.1000 call. A number that reads as a market is refused with the line rather than taken as a strike. A request that names something the market box also quoted carries that market beside our price, so inside their market survives the split; one nothing quoted is priced just the same, which is the whole reason for asking separately. A risk reversal asked for as JPY call over is quoted in that convention, and the row says so. Each stage reports separately, so one that cannot run leaves the other alone.
    The run may be written as columns. As well as broker English, a line can be expiry, strike, bid/offer — the middle column taking ATM, an absolute strike, or a delta (25d, 25dp, -25d), which is the same vocabulary as the pricing screen's Strike box. A bare 25d does not say which wing, so it is read as the call and the row says so; write 25dp or -25d for the put. An absolute strike needs no side at all — the volatility there is one number — but it does need a forward feed, because the surface works in strike over forward. A comma is a column boundary and a price never straddles one: that is what tells 3M, 7.75, 8.30 (a choice price at the 7.75 strike) from 3M 7.75 8.30 (the two-way at-the-money it has always been).
    A weekday is an intra-week expiry. fri ATM 8.15/8.55 is the next Friday from today and mon 1.1000 call the next Monday — the day names in full or from three letters (thu, thur, thurs). Always forward and never today: Fri read on a Friday is the Friday coming, because an option quoted this morning to expire this morning is not what a run means, and the row says which date it landed on. It is the weakest way to say when — a tenor or a date on the same line is used instead, with a note — and a day name in front of a time (mon 09:15 1M ATM …) is the day the run was written rather than an expiry.
    Timestamps, and what wins. A line may start with 09:15, 2024-02-28 09:15 or [09:15]. When two lines quote the same thing, the later timestamp wins whichever order they were pasted in; without timestamps to compare, the later line wins, because that is the only ordering an untimed line carries. The quote that lost is listed under the paste with the line that beat it, never silently dropped. It still counts as evidence when Learn widths measures the market: one tenor quoted twice is one live price and two observations of how wide it is shown. A time-only line takes the last date above it; a run with no date anywhere is ordered as one day, and says so, because that ordering is wrong across midnight.
    Conventions the paste decides once. A volatility is read as the number it was written as, in points: 8.20 is 8.20 points and 0.35 is a third of one, which is what a managed pair marks. The level never decides a unit, and a paste that mixes the two sizes is read rather than refused; a paste that really is in decimals is read by setting Vol unit to decimal, one scale for the whole paste and never line by line. A risk reversal with a direction word (EUR call over) is resolved against the pair; one without is read in the book's own convention, and the paste says so. An unqualified fly means whichever butterfly the selector above says, and the market strangle is the one the workbook marks.
    Shading the mid. A rich market and a long vega position both point the same way: both are reasons to want to sell, and you attract a seller's trade by shading the price down. Both are capped as a fraction of the width, so an axe can lean the price inside the market but cannot walk it out of the market on its own. Neither is applied to a risk reversal or a butterfly — a break-even against realized volatility and a vega position are statements about the level, and neither says where the skew belongs. Those rows say so.
    The knowledge bank is the desk's own file, kept beside the workbook. A spread sets the width, a floor is a minimum width, a shift moves the mid, and a note is prose that is shown beside the quote and never applied. The narrowest matching rule wins and every row names the rule that set it. There is no built-in default: a quote no rule matches gets no bid and no offer unless a fallback tier is chosen above, and says which it was.
    The fallback is a ladder, not a number. The bottom rung of the width ladder is a spreading tier — a column of the workbook's KACE_SPREADS tab, the same ladder the kACE feed posts from — read at each quote's own maturity. It used to be one typed width for every tenor on the screen, which is not a width any desk shows: a one-week and a one-year two-way are not the same width, so one box was either far too wide at the front or far too tight at the back. Tier × multiplies that ladder (the mid does not move, only the width) and Between tenors decides whether a maturity between two of the tab's tenors takes the nearer one's width or a width read straight across between them. Tiers are edited on the KACE_SPREADS table in the Config window, and because that is the same tab the feed reads, a width shown to a client and a width posted to the platform cannot quietly differ. Leaving the tier on (none) is the honest default: a quote the bank and the archive cannot account for gets no price rather than an invented one.
    A premium, and what a live line leaves unsaid. A price can be written as a premium rather than a volatility — pips, %, a currency word, or bp, which is a hundredth of a per cent of the base notional, so 5bp is 0.05% and never 5 of anything. It is inverted once, against the feed’s forward at that line’s own expiry, and the row shows the volatility it implies with the premium it came from beside it. A premium is dealt on a strike and normally needs its side written out, because a call and a put there are two different prices; the exception is live — an option dealt without its delta hedge, which is what makes it a low-delta option. It is the out-of-the-money one, so the side is read from the moneyness: above the forward the call, below it the put, said on the row. Read off a strike that turns out to be near the money, the row warns that this was a guess at a forty-delta option and asks for the word.
    The market table reads one line per quote. The message a line carries — warnings, the width’s source, what the agent suggested — sits under it and is shown on the hover; click the line to pin it open. A line that has one is marked with an ellipsis after its name, so nothing is hidden that was not announced. The Now column is coloured by where the mark sits in that line’s two-way: plain comfortably inside it, amber within Edge tol of a side, and in the alert colour when the mark is above their offer or below their bid. Hovering the number says how far, in vol points and in widths.
    Nothing on this screen touches the workbook. The check and the quote report and put the marks back, verified. What the marking card leaves on the loaded book with keep the marks is in memory only, and a reload discards it.
    A held set of marks is only good for the book it came from. Re-mark the pair on the Vol marking screen — a curve applied, a tenor pinned, a wing re-quoted, a band, an event — and what the marking card is holding is dropped rather than laid back over what you have just marked: the check and the quote read the book as it now stands and say which part moved. Propose, or fit again, to stand on a curve of this book.

    Smile parameters, as a field

    What these two maps are, and what the band between the rows is not Across is expiry, evenly spaced in √t — the axis a term structure is read on; linear in time spends most of the picture on the back end, where the parameters have stopped moving. The ticks are the pair's own quoted tenors. Every column is the surface at that expiry, so a marked term structure, a per-tenor overwrite, a shift and the anchor are all in the picture: the map cannot show a curve the surface is not on.
    Up is the wing the parameter was calibrated from — 10 delta at the bottom, 25 delta at the top. Those two rows are the surface's own numbers. Everything between them is a straight line drawn between two calibrations, not a model read: there is no such thing as rho at 17 delta. The band is there to show how far the two calibrations sit apart and whether that gap moves with expiry, which is what the two columns side by side in the grid are hard to read.
    The colour. rho is signed, so its map is diverging about zero — not about the middle of today's numbers — and a pair whose rho never changes sign shows one hue, which is the honest picture. slog is positive and reads off a floor, so its map is sequential in one hue over its own range. Both are stepped into bands with a line at each band edge, so a level can be followed across the map rather than guessed from a wash of colour. Hovering reads the value out.

    What these two files are The market feed is spot and forward points, and the <CCY>OIS rows beside them that discount a premium. Loading it here puts it under every screen at once; Refresh spot, watch file and auto-load stay on the pricing toolbar, because re-reading a publication during a morning is not a setting.

    The pairs this workbook builds and the tabs that configure it. A tab is marked, not written: Apply <tab> puts it into this session and reads the book again on top of it, so every screen shows what it does straight away and the workbook still says what it said. It reaches the file with the marks, under Write to workbook on the Vol marking screen — one write, one backup, one place to look for what changed. What it replaces is kept in <name>.backups/ beside it, and is listed under Versions below with a button to put it back. What this session has marked goes back on the book afterwards, so adding a pair no longer costs a morning.

Removing a pair takes it out of CONFIG only — its column and its sheet stay, so adding it back finds what it had.
